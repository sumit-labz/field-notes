How to write a monologue

Sep 1, 2026 · 2 min read · Story Writing

Listen 0:00
a borrowed voice reads this (AI-generated narration)

I am seeking guidance on how to write monologues. According to AI, it referred me to Truby's Anatomy of Story. I resisted, because I thought Truby is for structure, not dialogue. But when I confronted it, here's what it replied:

What Truby does give you is the skeleton — the moment-by-moment belief shifts that make a character's voice matter. A punchy monologue isn't about being clever with words; it's about a character believing one thing, then having that belief cracked open by what they're saying. The punchiness comes from the contradiction between what they want to believe and what they're actually admitting.

Currently I am writing a story premise, and I can feel the character and his weakness. What if my monologues are a form of soliloquy?

What if my monologues are a form of soliloquy?

My memory of someone talking to themselves in front of a mirror — this could be an amazing idea: write a story, and also write a monologue for it.

But how do I do that? I'm still at the chapter-1 premise stage, and there's no sign of a monologue developing yet. To answer this impatience, here's what AI responded:

Truby's structure has 22 steps. Here's the reality: You can attempt a rough monologue by Step 6–7 (character definition, needs/flaws, story events starting to chain), but it won't have the resonance yet. The voice will be there, but it'll feel generic. You'll have a solid, emotionally coherent monologue around Step 14–16 — that's where Truby gets into the character's core wound and the turning point. By then you know what the character must say because you know what belief gets shattered. The full book's payoff comes at Steps 18–22 — that's when you're layering irony, contradiction, and the specific way they say it. That's where punchy lives. That's where you can write like Robert Smith — vulnerable but sharp, admitting something while defending against it. Practical answer for you: Don't wait for the full 22 steps. Do this instead: Pick your character's core wound (roughly Step 6–7 territory) Write the moment where they name that wound (Step 14–16) Record it in your own voice, raw — that's your monologue prototype Score it (this is where Chion takes over — the music decides what stays and what cuts) That's a 2-3 day sprint, not a 40-day roadmap. Do you have a character or a wound you're sitting on right now?

Okay, I think I should trust the process of story development, and do some exercises for monologue development while I build the story.
