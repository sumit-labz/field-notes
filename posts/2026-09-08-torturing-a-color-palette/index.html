Torturing the Words "Color Palette" Into a Poster

Sep 8, 2026 · 4 min read · Typography

Listen 0:00
a borrowed voice reads this (AI-generated narration)

This is my first attempt at a typographic poster. The idea: why not design one for my own MVP, Stencil and Frame? I started with a piece of text I wanted to see as a poster, wrote it down, and did some research into why people lean on choosing colors first without giving real thought to the brand itself.

Then I realized how hard this process actually is. There are so many variables to control, and just getting to a typographic poster is a challenge in itself — it needs art direction as much as it needs type. So I decided to constrain everything, the way you would in music: if you pick a C major scale, you commit to it. I pulled out my book on typographic systems and looked at different layouts, and made myself choose only a few — axial, random — even though my brain wanted to play with more, like dilational. I had a hard time holding myself to that. I still tried some dilational and axial layouts on pen and paper.

Then I went to Claude to build thumbnails — more and more variations, different effects. The good thing that came out of working with Claude was realizing my sentence had a deeper meaning buried in it: a color palette. These three words had to be tortured to pull that meaning out visually. As I kept making thumbnails and applying effects, the random layout pushed me toward a real intention: this had to be made difficult to read. That was the point. Not an easy read — how much harder could I make it?

This had to be made difficult to read. That was the point.

I made more and more layouts this way, until I ran out of credits. I was disappointed, and realized I'd been using OpenAI's model when Claude's Sonnet would have done the job better. At some point it was clear I had enough thumbnails — the real question was how to turn one into a finished piece. Low on Claude Pro credits too, I had two options: hand-develop it in Photoshop or Affinity Designer, or find another model good at typography that could carry it the rest of the way.

I tried Google Gemini Pro first, then thought Nano Banana Pro might be the better fit. I had credits on OpenRouter and access to it there, but no idea how to actually use it through OpenRouter. Eventually I found a tool called Open WebUI and wired my OpenRouter key into it to get Nano Banana running. Getting image generation working in Open WebUI had its own early friction, since I didn't know the tool — that's probably its own post. But the short version: pick a model, give it the capabilities you want, and you can find your way through it.

Working with Nano Banana Pro, I could finally see different text treatments land, and tweak toward the style I actually wanted. I tried every paper, ink, and graffiti effect I could think of, plus what it suggested — gradual fading, erasure, combinations of both. At some point I realized the torture of "color palette" mattered more than any single effect: how do you make it beautiful and still feel like someone erased it?

It was hard to control the AI's output. There were moments I wished I could get inside the layout and tweak it by hand, but it's just an image — there aren't many levers. What worked in my favor was volume: generating a lot of thumbnails, numbering them, and developing the one that matched my taste. That's the real advantage of generative AI here. I don't think I'm good at thumbnailing yet — it's something I want to grow into — but generating with AI is actually helping me build that skill alongside it.

The torture idea paid off. I'm not sure it's the best poster I've made, but I'd love to put it on a future YouTube channel, or use it in a marketing campaign somewhere. One thing I'd improve: right now the hero is really an anti-hero — the color palette being destroyed — and it's taking up most of the emphasis, while the reason for the piece to exist isn't given enough space. There's probably a better compositional balance there.

Right now the hero is really an anti-hero.

Overall, this was hyperfixating — intensely so, to the point I couldn't step away from it for a long time. The generation-and-selection loop is such a good process to work in. I'm still not comfortable with thumbnailing, and I'd like to get better at it next time, and try other layouts too. Coming up with a good brief off a good piece of text is its own challenge when you're self-practicing. Brief, art direction, controlling the AI, and my own thumbnailing process — those are the things to work on for the next typography assignment.
